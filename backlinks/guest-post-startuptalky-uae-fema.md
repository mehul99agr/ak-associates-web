# What Indian Founders Get Wrong About FEMA When Raising Foreign Funding

Raising money from a foreign investor is one of the most exciting milestones for an Indian startup. It is also, in the author's experience advising early-stage companies, the point at which FEMA compliance mistakes are most commonly made - mistakes that surface two or three years later during due diligence for the next funding round, when they are far more expensive to fix.

This article covers the five FEMA compliance gaps that appear most frequently in Indian startups that have taken foreign investment, and what founders can do about them.

## A Quick Primer: Why FEMA Applies to Foreign Investment

The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA) governs all cross-border capital flows into and out of India. When a foreign investor - whether a US VC, an angel from Singapore, or a UAE family office - puts money into an Indian company, that transaction is regulated under FEMA. The rules are administered by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) through the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019.

The most important practical implication: foreign investment in Indian companies must be reported to the RBI, and the shares must be issued within a strict timeline of receiving the money. Failure to comply is a compoundable offence under FEMA, but "compoundable" does not mean costless - the compounding fee can run into lakhs, and more importantly, unresolved FEMA violations are a deal-breaker for institutional investors conducting due diligence.

## Mistake 1: Not Filing the FC-GPR Within 30 Days

When a foreign investor subscribes to shares of an Indian company, the company must file Form FC-GPR (Foreign Currency - Gross Provisional Return) with the RBI within 30 days of issuing the shares. This is an online filing made through the RBI's FIRMS portal.

The 30-day clock starts from the date of allotment of shares, not from the date of receipt of funds.

Many early-stage founders complete the fundraise, receive the money in the company's bank account, and file the Form FC-GPR months later when their CA points it out - or worse, when a subsequent investor's due diligence team flags it. Late filing requires a compounding application to the RBI and payment of a compounding fee. For amounts under INR 1 crore, the minimum fee is INR 5,000; for larger amounts, it scales with the violation period.

**What to do:** Put the FC-GPR filing on the same checklist as share allotment. It should be a simultaneous step, not an afterthought.

## Mistake 2: Issuing Shares After 60 Days of Receiving the Money

Under FEMA, a company that has received foreign investment must issue shares within 60 days of receipt of funds. If shares are not issued within 60 days, the money must be refunded.

The most common scenario where this is violated: a startup receives a bridge loan or convertible note from a foreign investor, intends to convert it into equity "once the valuation is sorted," and takes four or five months to close the paperwork. During that window, the money sitting in the company's account is in violation of FEMA unless the instrument is structured correctly as a Foreign Currency Convertible Bond (FCCB) or an External Commercial Borrowing (ECB), both of which have their own compliance requirements.

**What to do:** Do not receive foreign money without knowing exactly which FEMA instrument it falls under. A convertible note is not automatically FEMA-compliant. Get CA advice before money moves.

## Mistake 3: Getting the Valuation Certificate Wrong

All foreign investment in Indian companies (except in sectors with government approval route) must be at a price that is not less than the fair value of the shares as determined by a SEBI-registered merchant banker or a Chartered Accountant using a recognised valuation method. The most commonly used method for startups is the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) method or the comparable company method under Rule 11UA of the Income Tax Rules.

Two common errors:
- Using a valuation report prepared by someone who is not a SEBI-registered merchant banker or a CA holding a valid certificate of practice (COP).
- The date of the valuation certificate is more than 90 days before the date of actual share allotment. RBI expects the valuation to be contemporaneous with the allotment.

**What to do:** Get a fresh valuation certificate for every round, dated within 90 days of allotment, from a qualified CA or SEBI merchant banker.

## Mistake 4: Ignoring the Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets (FLA Return)

Every Indian company that has received foreign investment (or has made overseas investment) must file an Annual Return on Foreign Liabilities and Assets (FLA Return) with the RBI by July 15 each year. This is a separate filing from the FC-GPR and is required every year as long as the foreign investment is outstanding on the company's balance sheet.

Many startups file the FC-GPR correctly at the time of the investment and then stop there, not realising that the FLA Return is a recurring obligation. Missing the FLA Return is a FEMA violation, and RBI does send notices for non-filing - particularly as it has improved cross-referencing between its FIRMS portal and MCA21 data.

**What to do:** Add the FLA Return to your annual statutory compliance calendar alongside your income tax return and ROC filings.

## Mistake 5: Assuming a SAFE Agreement is Automatically Compliant

Simple Agreements for Future Equity (SAFEs) have become popular in the Indian startup ecosystem, partly because of their simplicity in the US context. In India, a SAFE from a foreign investor is a cross-border instrument, and its FEMA treatment depends on how it is structured.

An improperly structured SAFE can be treated by the RBI as a loan (debt instrument) rather than an equity investment, which has different compliance requirements - and in some cases, sector-specific caps on foreign debt. The RBI has issued clarifications on convertible instruments, but the nuances matter.

**What to do:** Any convertible instrument from a foreign investor should be reviewed by a CA or lawyer familiar with FEMA before it is signed. The fact that a format is standard in Silicon Valley does not make it automatically compliant in India.

## The Underlying Pattern

All five mistakes above share a common root cause: FEMA compliance is treated as a post-transaction step rather than a pre-transaction checkpoint. The right approach is to involve a CA familiar with FEMA at the term sheet stage - not after the money has arrived.

The cost of getting FEMA compliance right at the outset is a fraction of the cost of compounding violations two years later when a Series A investor's due diligence team finds them.
